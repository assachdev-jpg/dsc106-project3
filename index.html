Where Will Warm the Fastest?

Each square is a 5° × 5° patch of the planet. Colors show how much warmer its near-surface air is than the 1850–1900 average, averaged over each decade. Data: CMIP6 climate model GFDL-ESM4, historical run (1850–2014) joined with two future scenarios (2015–2100).

Region and world averages are area-weighted, because grid squares near the poles cover much less of the Earth's surface than squares near the equator.

Why we made this

We decided we wanted to learn about climate change and whether it warms each part of Earth by the same amount or if it's different depending on the location. When you look at social media, the news, etc. you're typically given a single global number about global warming, like +2°C, but is that average consistent everywhere? In the climate model we ended up using, there is a high-emissions and a low-emissions scenario. Under high emissions, the world warms by about 3.9°C by the 2090s, and the Arctic specifically warms by about 7.8°C, which is about twice as much. Since we also wanted to show that emissions play a role, in the low-emissions scenario the world only warms by about 1.4°C instead of 3.9°C. The question we want to answer is: where will warm the fastest, and how much does that depend on the emissions path we take?

Design decisions

We used the GFDL-ESM4 model from CMIP6, using near-surface air temperature from the historical run (1850-2014) joined with 2 future scenarios (2015-2100): SSP1-2.6 (low emissions) and SSP5-8.5 (high emissions). We regridded the data to 5° by 5° squares and averaged each square over decades, which smooths out outlier years and keeps the file small enough to load quickly in the browser. Instead of using raw temperatures, we show the change from each square's own 1850-1900 average. Raw temperatures would just show that the poles are cold and the equator is hot, which is what we'd expect, and would hide the warming we're interested in. When we average a region or the whole world, we weight each square by the cosine of its latitude, because squares closer to the poles cover less of Earth than squares closer to the equator.

We decided to use a map because showing position on a map is the most natural and easiest way for viewers to understand "where." Each square is colored by how much it has warmed using a sequential yellow-to-red scale with binned steps (0-1°C, 1-2°C, etc.). We used bins instead of a continuous gradient because it makes it easier to read the exact range from the legend, and the bins line up with simple targets people already understand. Squares that cooled get a separate blue color so they don't blend in with small amounts of warming. We used an equirectangular projection so every grid square stays a rectangle, which keeps the grid honest. The tradeoff is that it makes the poles look bigger than they really are, which is why we area-weight the averages instead of relying on what the map looks like.

The visualization also has interactive components. The decade slider lets viewers watch the warming build up over time, and the scenario toggle lets them compare the two futures for the same place. You can drag a box on the map to pick any region, or use the preset buttons (Arctic, United States, Europe, etc.) as starting points. The selected region is linked to the line chart below, which shows that region and the world average from 1850 to 2100, with the scenario you're not viewing faded out so the two futures can be compared without too much going on. Dotted lines mark the 1.5°C and 2°C Paris Agreement targets so viewers have a reference point. Hovering over a square shows its exact warming under both scenarios. With a normal static map, you could only show one decade and one scenario at a time, so the interactions let viewers explore and discover patterns on their own. One limitation is that we only used one climate model, and GFDL-ESM4 is on the cooler end of the CMIP6 models, so other models would likely show even more warming.

Development process

We split the work among our three group members: Aarman, Chase, and Elias. Aarman started with processing the CMIP6 dataset and building the interactive map with the different emissions scenarios and the decade slider. Elias focused on creating the line chart that compares regional warming to the global average and helping connect it to the map. Chase worked on the website layout, the region selection features, and making sure the visualization was easy to navigate and understand. We all worked together on testing the interactive features and fixing bugs.

We estimate that we spent around 25 people-hours total on the project. The most time-consuming part was getting the line chart and map to work together, especially making sure that changing the selected region or emissions scenario updated both visualizations correctly. Pulling the CMIP6 data also took a while, since the code kept breaking on the climate model's calendar format, and then processing it so it could load smoothly on the website took more time on top of that.
